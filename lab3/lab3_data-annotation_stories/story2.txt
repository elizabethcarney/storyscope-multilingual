Riya had agreed to the project for the most practical of reasons, which is to say she had not agreed to it at all, not really. It had descended upon her in stages—first as an email from the Institute of Littoral Memory in Karun Port, then as a corridor conversation with her department head, then as a signed field authorization slipped under her office door with the quiet menace of bureaucratic inevitability. By the time she stood on the ferry to ThuLadvipa’s eastern interior, notebook damp with sea mist and stomach cool with resentment, everyone around her spoke as if the matter had long ago been settled.

She was, they said, uniquely suited.

Riya disagreed.

The project title alone made her tired: Preliminary Survey on Surviving Thuri Epidermal Markings and Associated Ritual Practices in the Central Villages of ThuLadvipa. It was the sort of title that implied grant committees, earnest reports, and months of asking old people to relive what history had already punished them for remembering. The island’s Thuri body tattoos—once worn as signs of vow, kinship, fertility, mourning, discipline, craft, and oath—had been prohibited in one era, romanticized in another, commercialized in a third, and now sat in the stale museum language of endangered intangible heritage. Researchers came and went. Photographs were catalogued. Motifs were compared. Papers were written with words like liminal and syncretic and post-erasure. Yet very little had changed for the people whose skin had borne the original archive.

Riya was twenty-nine, trained in epigraphy and comparative oral systems, happiest among inscriptions that did not answer back. Bodies unsettled her research habits. Bodies changed, healed, aged, refused legibility. The island’s elders did not owe her their arms, shoulders, backs, or stories. She knew that, and perhaps because she knew it, she had no wish to become another careful thief.

But the Institute wanted a younger scholar with local language competence and family roots in ThuLadvipa’s interior districts. Riya’s grandmother had been from the island. Her surname opened doors in certain villages and closed them in others. She could read old harbor ledgers, temple wall scripts, plantation censuses, and the riverine prayer rolls stored in damp boxes under district shrines. “You’re not just cataloguing designs,” her department head had said. “You can contextualize them.”

“I do not want to contextualize anyone’s skin,” Riya had replied.

“Then contextualize the meanings around it.”

As if meanings stood apart from flesh.

Now the ferry pushed through a sheet of pewter water toward a coastline layered in haze. ThuLadvipa rose gradually, not as cliffs but as terraces of green and stone and weather-softened villages. Above it all, visible because it was always visible, hung the Spaceship.

It was never called anything else by those who had grown up under it. Outsiders wrote articles trying to name it: orbital relic, derelict vessel, atmospheric station, celestial artifact. Religious tracts gave it titles drenched in inevitability. Schoolchildren drew it simply as the Ship, a long dark crescent with a segmented spine, fixed in the same impossible part of the sky for so many generations that the argument over whether it was moving had become metaphysical rather than astronomical. At dawn it glimmered faint silver. At noon it seemed charred. On rainy days it vanished into cloud and people looked up too often, as if to check whether absence meant departure.

The oldest stories said it had arrived soundlessly in the era before the unification of the island polities. The second oldest said it had always been there and only became visible when the language of the island fractured. The more recent stories were practical: some said it was dead machinery; some said sleeping gods; some said a prison; some said a mirror so distant it reflected a future not yet reached. Scientists from abroad had come with instruments and left with inconclusive data and a chastened respect for humidity. The Ship orbited and did not descend. It cast no shadow any human eye had ever tracked. Yet it altered everything simply by enduring.

Riya had spent her childhood beneath it and no longer thought of that endurance as wondrous. It was like weather, empire, memory, debt: too old to astonish, too strange to resolve.

She disembarked at Karun Port and crossed the city in a hired auto whose canvas roof snapped in the wind. The port district smelled of salt, engine oil, frying lentil batter, wet rope, and the medicinal bitterness of drying sea herbs. Old warehouses leaned toward the water under murals half-faded by monsoon wash—merchant ships, guardian eels, women with painted shoulders holding lamps under the hovering Ship. Riya caught herself looking too long at one mural where the women’s tattoo lines rose from collarbone to neck in tightly nested arcs. A note of professional guilt stirred. Already she was seeing patterns.

The Institute occupied a former customs house with coral-stone walls and a courtyard full of broken cannons repurposed as planters. In the archive room, boxes awaited her with labels written in three different hands over four decades. Survey photographs. District reports. Untranslated interviews. Rubbings of clay tablets found in shrine foundations. Sketchbooks from colonial administrators who had treated tattooed women and men as decorative anthropology. Riya spent two days reviewing enough to confirm what she had suspected: almost everything in the formal record was fragmented.

The tattoos, called thuri-markings in the translated reports, had once been categorized into broad regions of the body. Wrist and forearm bands tied to apprenticeship. Rib and flank grids associated with childbirth vows. Back-shoulder constellations for navigators and fisher lineages. Neckline motifs for singers, ritual attendants, and mourners. But among the motifs recurring in old sketches were signs no one had satisfactorily explained. Not floral. Not geometric in any purely ornamental sense. They clustered in repeating sequences. Some appeared mirrored across the body’s central axis. Others shifted placement according to age or life event. A retired district officer had scribbled in the margin of one notebook: “Resembles notation? Perhaps liturgical? Ask Vaarta families.”

Vaarta.

The word snagged in her attention.

In current speech, vaarta was used loosely to mean story, record, family account, transmitted saying. But in older documents the capitalization appeared in translation notes as if the term had once indicated something more formal, perhaps a hereditary role or corpus: Vaarta houses, Vaarta keepers, Vaarta recitation rights. Many of those references clustered in inland districts where mission schools and later state reforms had most aggressively discouraged body marking and local ritual practice. The line between archive and wound blurred again.

On the third morning, as she was copying an index card, her phone vibrated. Amma.

Riya stared at the name longer than she needed to before answering.

“Have you reached?” her mother asked, by which she meant not the city but the state of emotional arrival that mothers detect like weather pressure.

“I reached two days ago.”

“And you did not call.”

“I was settling in.”

“You were avoiding me.”

Riya smiled despite herself. “Also that.”

Her mother made the soft click of disapproval she reserved for truths offered too easily. In the background Riya heard a pressure cooker hiss, a radio announcer flattening vowels in the capital accent, and the small domestic percussion of metal lids. Her mother lived on the mainland now, in a university apartment that held no sea salt in its walls and had never looked up at the Ship except during brief visits. Yet some part of her remained aligned to the island’s interior villages with the precision of migratory instinct.

“You’re going to Purja,” her mother said.

Riya straightened. “How do you know about Purja?”

“Because if they are sending you for Thuri markings, eventually they will send you where your grandmother was born. Better to go first.”

“That’s not in my assigned circuit.”

“Then alter the circuit.”

“Amma—”

“Listen to me.” Her mother’s tone shifted. Not dramatic. Simply firmer, quieter. “Go to Purja. See your mother’s mother’s house if it still stands, though I doubt the roof has survived. Ask for Nalin auntie near the east well if she is alive, or her son if she is not. There is a teak chest wrapped in old blue silk. If it has not been sold, bring me the seals.”

“The what?”

“The stamp-seals. Your grandmother called them hand-stamps, but they are not for hands. You will know them.”

Riya leaned against the archive table. Outside the louvered windows the sea wind moved papers in tiny tides. “Amma, I’ve never heard about any seals.”

“No,” her mother said. “Because no one spoke of them after your grandfather moved us away. And because I was told not to.”

That answer contained enough old sediment to silence Riya.

Her mother continued more gently. “When I was little, my mother kept them with her wedding silk and mourning cloths. We were not to touch them. Once I asked why, and she said, ‘They remember what skin forgot.’ I thought she was being poetic. Later I was told they belonged to our Vaarta line.”

Riya almost interrupted, but the word held her still.

“We are not a Vaarta line,” she said at last.

“Perhaps not now,” her mother replied. “Perhaps not in any way that officials would recognize. Go to Purja.”

“Why didn’t you tell me any of this before?”

A pause. Then: “Because I did not know what was memory, what was family vanity, and what was danger. Because you were a child under that thing in the sky, and the adults around you had enough unfinished fear. Because every family on ThuLadvipa keeps one locked room of history and calls it prudence. Is that enough honesty for one phone call?”

Riya closed her eyes. “Barely.”

“Good. Then go.”

After the call, Riya sat among the boxes without moving. The archive room smelled of paper mold and lampblack. Somewhere above the ceiling a pigeon walked with officious taps. We are not a Vaarta line, she had said automatically, with the certainty of inherited denial. But the certainty itself now seemed suspicious. Families often most forcefully disowned what they had only partially buried.

That afternoon she revised her route.

Purja lay inland from Karun Port, beyond the brackish paddy belt and the tamarind ridges, where the roads narrowed into laterite tracks veined with roots. She hired a jeep and driver named Sefu, who wore a green cap advertising an obsolete telecom company and spoke in cheerful bursts punctuated by long practical silences. The city gave way to villages painted in limewash blues and turmeric yellows, then to low shrines under banyans, then to flooded fields where egrets stepped with priestly care. Women in rolled saris bent over rice shoots. Children waved from embankments. At every turn of the road the Ship shifted only enough to prove it had not shifted at all.

Sefu drove with one hand on the wheel and the other drumming the dashboard. “Purja is quiet now,” he said. “Mostly old people and those too stubborn to leave. Was a weaving village once. Good silk. Also singers, I think. My grandmother said they marked the brides there differently. Around the ribs, very fine lines.”

“Do people still remember the meanings?”

“Remember?” He shrugged. “They remember that there were meanings. Same as everyone.”

The inland air was warmer, richer with damp earth and fermenting jackfruit. Near sunset they passed a hillside where stone posts emerged from grass in rows, each carved with weathered spirals and ladder-like grooves. Riya asked Sefu to stop. The posts stood perhaps chest-high, some toppled, some upright. Not graves exactly, though she had seen them recorded as funerary markers. The grooves on one post echoed a motif from an old tattoo sketch: three descending bars cut by a hook.

She photographed them, then felt the old researcher’s shame at extracting surfaces from place. Yet the place itself was gone in all but fragments. Documentation and theft, preservation and trespass—she had never found clean boundaries.

Purja announced itself not with a sign but with absence. The road narrowed between old mango trees. Houses appeared at irregular distances, each set back behind low walls draped in vines. Many roofs had been repaired with corrugated metal. The village pond lay green and still under a swarm of dragonflies. There was, as Sefu had warned, a prevailing quiet—not emptiness exactly, but a quiet that had settled where a denser sound once lived. Looms no longer clacked. No ceremonial drums drifted from courtyards. Even the dogs barked briefly and gave up.

The east well stood beside a peepal tree split by lightning and still leafing. There, on a charpoy under woven shade mats, sat a woman so small and straight-backed she seemed to have been folded out of cane. Her hair was entirely white, parted with uncompromising precision. Her forearms were dark, thin, and marked.

Riya saw the tattoos before she saw the woman’s face.

They climbed from wrist to elbow in interlocking bands of dots, chevrons, stepped curves, and tiny rectangular notches. Age had blurred some edges, but the density remained startling. This was not ornament in the casual sense. It was arrangement. Sequence.

The woman looked up as Riya approached. Her eyes were pale brown and unsparing.

“You are Lata’s daughter,” she said.

Riya stopped. “Yes.”

“Nalin auntie is dead. I am her sister Mira. Sit down before you start asking city questions.”

Sefu, recognizing a dismissal meant for all excess urbanity, retreated discreetly to smoke near the jeep.

Mira poured water from a brass lota into a steel tumbler and handed it over. Her own hands bore no tremor. The skin between thumb and forefinger was stamped with a faded circular form unlike the linear bands on her arm.

“I’m here on Institute work,” Riya began.

“Of course you are. Otherwise your mother would have kept you away.”

“I’m documenting surviving Thuri tattoos, with consent—”

Mira clicked her tongue. “You all say consent now as if the word itself heals history.”

Riya accepted the rebuke. “I also came because my mother asked me to look for something in my grandmother’s house. A teak chest. Wrapped in blue silk. Stamp-seals.”

Mira regarded her for a long moment, then laughed once, softly and without amusement. “So Lata remembered after all.”

“She remembered enough to send me.”

“Dangerous age, enough.”

Mira rose more easily than Riya expected and beckoned. “Come, then. Your grandmother’s house is trying to become soil but has not succeeded yet.”

The house stood at the edge of the village behind a half-collapsed wall overgrown with night-blooming jasmine. The roof had indeed failed in sections. Sunlight entered through broken tiles and lay in angled bars across dust. Yet the central room still held the geometry of habitation: a niche for lamps, a blackened cooking alcove, a low shelf where ceramic jars sat undisturbed beneath spider silk. Riya’s grandmother had died when Riya was five. Her memories were fragmentary—betel-stained lips, the roughness of unstarched cotton, a song without words hummed while shelling peas. She had not known this house well enough to grieve it, but standing inside its slow ruin she felt a tug deeper than sentiment, as if architecture itself recognized her blood more confidently than she recognized it.

Mira went directly to the inner room. “Here.”

Against the wall under a fallen beam sat a teak chest dark with age, brass corners greened by damp. On top of it lay folded cloths protected under oilskin. Mira removed the covering layer by layer: an indigo shawl, a striped wrap, and finally a length of blue silk gone almost gray at the creases.

Inside the chest were things that had survived by being unfashionable: a wedding blouse embroidered in tarnished silver thread; packets of dried herbs turned to powder; a string of black glass beads; a wooden comb missing half its teeth; notebooks in a script Riya could not immediately place because mildew had laced the ink; and beneath them all, nested in a smaller lacquered box, seven stamp-seals.

Riya lifted one carefully.

It fit the palm like a river stone. The handle was carved from dark wood, polished by repeated use. The stamping face appeared to be bronze or some copper alloy, its relief pattern astonishingly fine: concentric arcs crossed by three vertical interruptions and a hooked tail. Another seal bore a field of minute squares enclosed in a spiral frame. Another, a ladder motif enclosed by dots. Each was different, but all possessed the precision of formal design rather than casual craft. Their edges were dark with residue.

“Ink?” Riya murmured.

Mira shook her head. “Paste. Soot, oil, crushed leaf, sometimes powdered shell. Pressed before the needle work.”

Riya looked up. “These were tattoo guides.”

“Guides, yes. Sometimes temporary marks for ritual days when no needle was used. Sometimes teaching tools. Sometimes records.” Mira reached out and tapped one seal with a nail. “This one your grandmother would never let us children play with. She said it was speech.”

The room seemed to tighten around the word.

Riya arranged the seals on a folded cloth and photographed them from several angles, then stopped, ashamed at the reflex to flatten discovery into data before understanding had even begun. “You knew about these?”

“I knew they existed. Not all that they meant.” Mira lowered herself onto a stool. “By my girlhood, much had already gone. The state inspectors had come years before. Mission women before them. Reformers after. Everyone wanting our skins either covered or explained. Men also stopped the old practices because work in the city paid better than memory. Some motifs continued in secret. Most became wedding decoration painted with rice paste. Safer to wash away.”

“And Vaarta?” Riya asked.

Mira’s expression changed. Not exactly softened, but turned inward. “Your grandmother’s mother was from a Vaarta house. Not one of the greatest ones, if such vanity matters to scholars. A line of keepers from the inland shrine circuits. They were not priests. Do not confuse those things. Vaarta people carried patterned knowledge between villages. Genealogies, vow sequences, mourning permissions, navigation chants for inland rivers and coastal stars, agreements between craft houses, the order of offerings during flood years, the old names for winds. What could not always be written, they held. Some in song. Some in cloth. Some in skin.”

Riya sat very still.

“The body tattoos,” Mira continued, “were never just decoration. You know this already or they would not have sent you. But most people now imagine each motif stood alone—this for marriage, that for fishing, another for grief. They forget combinations. Placement changed reading. Sequence changed meaning. Left arm differed from right. Spine differed from ribs. Some markings told what vows had been taken. Some told what duties could be performed. Some warned what griefs had been survived. Some identified who could receive which recitations. And a few…” She glanced toward the open roof where a wedge of sky showed. “…a few were said to be portions of an older script.”

Riya’s pulse quickened. “A script? As in a writing system?”

Mira spread her hands. “As in what old people say when younger people have abandoned the habit of listening.”

Outside, a koel called once from the mango tree and fell silent.

Riya took out her notebook. “Can you show me your arm? Only if you want.”

Mira extended her forearm without self-consciousness. Up close the pattern resolved into columns interrupted by clusters. Not random repetition, but structured variation. Some units recurred at measured intervals. Others appeared only once. Riya sketched quickly, then more carefully, trying to preserve proportion rather than aesthetic impression. The dots were not filler; they aligned with breaks. The notches resembled the carved stone posts they had passed on the road. On the inner arm a narrow band contained seven repeated marks, each altered by a single extension.

“How old were you when this was done?”

“Eight for the first bands. Twelve for the next. Fifteen when my grandmother insisted on the upper row before the ban tightened. She said if we lost all reading from skin we would become easy prey to every clerk and preacher. She was right.”

“Can you read it?”

“Pieces. Enough to know what I have forgotten.”

The answer pierced Riya more sharply than a refusal would have.

That night Mira insisted Riya sleep in her house rather than return to the nearest lodge two villages away. Sefu left with promises to come back in two days. Dinner was rice, pumpkin cooked with fenugreek, fried river fish, and curd set in a clay bowl. After the meal, as insects built a high electric chorus beyond the screened windows, Mira brought out a kerosene lamp and the old notebooks from the chest. Their pages were brittle but not beyond rescue. The script was unfamiliar at first because it sat between systems: some curves reminiscent of the coastal mercantile hand, some angular forms older, several ligatures she had never seen. Between passages appeared small stamped impressions in fading black. Seal marks.

Riya bent over them until the lamp heat dried her eyes.

“Your grandmother copied from older cloth records,” Mira said. “When officials searched houses, paper was easier to hide than silk panels.”

“What happened to the cloth records?”

“Burned, hidden, used as dowry wrapping, eaten by rats, taken by collectors. The usual history.”

Riya traced one stamped sign in the margin without touching it. The same spiral frame as one of the bronze seals, but inside it not squares now—something altered, a diagonal cut. Annotation? inflection? gendered variation? She forced herself not to leap too quickly into conjecture. Yet the philologist in her had awakened with dangerous force. This was not merely body ornament. This was patterned symbolic transmission with combinatory logic. If the seals mapped onto tattoo sequences and notebook notations, they might preserve more than ritual categories. They might encode syntax.

Or they might tempt her into imposing script where symbol sufficed. Scholars had made that mistake often, particularly when desperate to discover literacy in forms they had previously ignored. She wrote in the margin of her notebook: Do not romanticize. Test recurrence. Test positional shifts. Compare with extant inscriptions.

Mira watched her over the lamp flame. “Your face has changed.”

“In what way?”

“You have stopped looking bored.”

Riya laughed quietly. “I was never bored.”

“You were professionally unwilling. Different disease.”

Near midnight, when the lamp had burned low, Mira said, “Do you know why some people connect the Vaarta with the Ship?”

Riya looked up. “No.”

“Because of silence.”

“That explains very little.”

“It explains almost everything on this island.” Mira settled her shawl around her shoulders. “The oldest tale I heard was that when the Ship first appeared, it made no thunder, no fire, no voice. It simply took a place in the sky and watched. The island’s rulers demanded interpretation from priests, navigators, foreigners, madmen, anyone. Many spoke. None agreed. Then the Vaarta houses began reciting a sequence called the Listening of Heights. Not to call the Ship down—do not put childishness into old practices—but to align the island’s many speech traditions, because confusion was becoming violence. Some say the body markings expanded in that period. More signs were added. The keepers needed a way to carry stable patterns across dialects, storms, and political borders. Skin cannot be confiscated as easily as paper. Also, skin is remembered by touch.”

Riya thought of the stamped seals, the notebooks, the old carvings by the roadside. “And the language was lost?”

“Languages are not birds that fly away all at once,” Mira said. “They are shorelines. They erode, they silt over, they are renamed on maps. What was lost may be a register, not a whole tongue. Or a way of ordering words. Or the agreement that these signs pointed to speech rather than merely custom.” She gave Riya a pointed look. “You scholars love clean funerals. Real disappearance is muddier.”

Rain came before dawn, heavy and warm, drumming through the broken tiles of the abandoned house and on Mira’s repaired roof like a thousand fingers. Riya woke on a woven cot under a mosquito net and for a moment did not know where she was. Then the village sounds arranged themselves: rainwater filling jars, a goat shaking itself, someone sweeping a courtyard despite the futility of sweeping in rain.

Over the next two days Purja opened to her in increments. News traveled quickly. Once villagers learned she was Lata’s daughter and was asking, carefully, about old markings, some came to the veranda to look at the seals; others sent messages declining but blessing the inquiry; a few appeared only at dusk, spoke in fragments, and left before she could record names. She did not pressure anyone. That alone seemed to surprise them.

An old weaver named Bheema showed her a chest panel used in bride processions, on which certain border motifs matched the seals precisely. “These were once stamped in rice paste on the bride’s side before weaving the final hem,” he said. “My mother knew which sequence meant crossing households with protection and which meant leaving one lineage debt unpaid. We simplified after the troubles.”

A widower called Harun uncovered his late sister’s ceremonial blouse, the inside seams stitched with tiny repeating signs invisible when worn. “For the skin underneath,” he said. “Not to show.”

At the pond, a woman in her seventies named Devaki rolled up her sari blouse sleeve and revealed a fading shoulder cluster of nested crescents. “This one was for mourning,” she said. “But not death. Separation without funeral. When my husband left for the mainland and never sent word, my aunt marked me. She said there must be a sign for griefs the law does not recognize.”

Riya copied, compared, and listened. Patterns began to recur across media: tattoo to textile, textile to seal, seal to notebook margin, margin to carved post. More strikingly, some motifs appeared only in paired relation, as if one modified the other. A line of seven notches near the elbow corresponded to a stamped sequence in a notebook passage that also contained repeated phonetic markers in a gloss added later in common script. She spent hours constructing provisional tables.

At first the work felt exhilarating in the tidy way of intellectual pursuit. Then, as she recognized the family names attached to certain motifs and found her own grandmother’s lineage among the cross-references, exhilaration tilted into unease. The archive was no longer external. One notebook page included a list of households entitled—if her tentative reading was right—Permitted Bearers During Eclipse Rites. Three generations up, her great-great-grandmother’s name appeared beside a seal pattern now resting in her own lap.

That evening she called her mother.

Amma answered on the second ring. “You found them.”

“Yes.”

A silence full of vindicated caution.

“You knew we were named in the records,” Riya said.

“I suspected.”

“You suspected enough to send me into a ruined house to discover a hereditary role no one mentioned for thirty years.”

“I sent you because if the seals remained there, they were rotting. And because if anyone outside the family found them first, they would become exhibit material.”

Riya went to the courtyard so Mira would not overhear the edge in her voice. The rain had cleared. Frogs pulsed in the wet dark. Above the trees the Ship was a dim blade through cloud gaps.

“Were you ashamed of it?” Riya asked. “Of being connected to the Vaarta?”

Her mother exhaled slowly. “Not ashamed. Suspicious.”

“Of your own family?”

“Of what families do with prestige attached to loss. On the mainland, when people learned my mother came from a Vaarta line, they would become reverent in that ugly way educated people do. They wanted me to produce authenticity for them. A song, a recipe, a proverb, a tragic anecdote. Your grandfather hated it. He believed survival required ordinary obscurity. And perhaps he was right. But obscurity becomes erasure if practiced too long.”

Riya leaned her head against the damp wall. “There are patterns here that may be linguistic.”

“Yes,” her mother said quietly. “My mother hinted as much when she thought I was too young to understand. She said the old markings were not just read, they were sounded. Different tempos on the skin. Touch this line, pause, touch that dot cluster, reverse at the shoulder. She would trace my arm and recite things I did not know were lessons.”

“Why didn’t you learn properly?”

“Because by then she feared teaching what she could not protect. Because school punished village speech. Because I wanted books that opened the world and thought anything inherited must be a chain.” A rueful note entered her voice. “You, of all people, should understand that mistake.”

Riya did.

When she returned to the veranda, Mira looked at her once and said, “Mothers are always late with the crucial truth.”

On the third day Sefu returned, but Riya sent him away with extra money and a request to come back the following week. Purja, which she had expected to visit for a day, became her base. She set up a worktable in Mira’s spare room. Every morning she photographed seal impressions made in clay and lampblack, trying different pressures to understand line depth. She compared notebook passages against motif sequences on cloth and skin. She requested scans from the Institute of old district records mentioning Vaarta houses. At night she sat with Mira and whoever else wandered in to talk, not always about tattoos. Flood years. The grain shortage under emergency rule. How the village singers stopped using a certain scale after the west shrine collapsed. Which children now living in the city still dreamed in Purja speech. Scholarship advanced through tea and digression more reliably than through official questionnaires.

The breakthrough arrived, as breakthroughs often do, by accident amplified through obsession.

Riya had been examining a repeated pattern on one of the seals: spiral frame, four interior squares, one square split diagonally. She had assumed the split indicated variation within a semantic category. But while reviewing photographs of roadside carved posts, she noticed the same interior-square arrangement beside what old survey notes identified as “flood height memorials.” On another post from a different district, the unsplit four-square mark appeared beside a date in a later calendar notation. Quantity? season? rank? Unsatisfying. She spread all instances across the floor.

Mira, passing with a tray of tea, glanced down. “Those are not squares,” she said.

Riya looked up. “What?”

“Not if you read them properly. They are folded mouths.”

“Folded—”

Mira set the tray down and crouched with a sigh indicating the spectacular incompetence of younger generations. She took a pencil and, instead of tracing the shape as seen from above, rotated the page ninety degrees and drew the motif as a stylized profile: upper lip, lower lip, closure line. Four mouths in sequence, one cut open. “My grandmother used to tap this on the table when someone broke recitation order. Closed, closed, closed, open. It means release after holding.”

Riya stared.

Not decorative quadrants. Mouth states. A notation of phonation? breath? vocal closure?

Her mind raced back through the notebook passages. Repeated clusters near certain glossed syllables. The split-mouth sign preceding a sequence transcribed by a later hand as “tha-ra-i.” Another passage containing alternating closed and open mouth forms next to a list of kinship names. Could the system encode not whole words but performance instructions—how to voice, pause, aspirate, sustain? A mnemotechnic bridge between speech and body placement?

She pulled another sheet toward her. “And this ladder mark?”

Mira touched her own sternum. “Climb.”

“As in rising pitch?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes rank. Sometimes direction of tracing on the skin. I told you, combinations matter.”

The room seemed to tilt. If the motifs carried layered information—semantic category, vocal instruction, bodily sequence—then the old assumption that Thuri tattoos were static symbols had obscured an entire expressive system. Not a script in the narrow bureaucratic sense perhaps, but a notation ecology linking body, speech, touch, and ritual action. A language technology.

For three days after that, Riya barely slept. She built tables of correspondences. Mouth forms, ladder marks, notch counts, dot clusters, hook tails. Positional overlays. She compared them with fragments of an otherwise undeciphered inscription held in the Institute basement, long classified as “ornamental border text” from a collapsed observatory shrine near the north cape. The same hook-tail sequence appeared there next to a carved depiction of the Ship.

Her scalp prickled.

She requested the observatory squeezes and photographs by urgent message. While waiting, she returned to the notebooks. One page in her grandmother’s hand contained a heading she could now partially parse: High Listening Pattern / For Clear Nights / Not During Storm Wind. Beneath it were stamped impressions and a brief gloss in later common script: “trace shoulders / speak upward / no answer expected.”

No answer expected.

Something in that phrase struck her with such force that she had to put the page down.

All her life the mystery of the Ship had been narrated as failed contact. Why did it not speak? Why did it not descend? Why did it watch? But what if one old island tradition had not centered response at all? What if the relevant practice was not summoning but alignment—ordering speech, body, and memory beneath a silence too large to interpret directly? The Ship as interlocutor had dominated modern imagination. The Ship as fixed reference point, a celestial constant around which human languages reorganized themselves, was stranger and somehow more plausible.

When the observatory images arrived, grainy but usable, she spread them alongside the seals. The carved panel depicted a long crescent object over stepped waves. Beneath it ran a band of signs dismissed for decades as decorative. Riya saw now the mouth forms, the ladder, the hook-tail, the pause dots. A recurring cluster matched the stamp-seal her grandmother had guarded most closely.

“Mira,” she called, voice unsteady. “Come look.”

Mira studied the printout, then lifted her chin toward the sky. “North cape shrine. My uncle went there once before it fell. Said the walls had skin on them.”

“Skin?”

“Rows of signs cut shallow, as if not to wound the stone too deeply.” She tapped the recurring cluster. “Can you read any of it?”

“Not read. Not fully. But maybe perform.” Riya swallowed. “I think this sequence corresponds to the Listening of Heights you mentioned. Or one variant. The glosses suggest a ritual tracing order—shoulders, throat, sternum—and vocal closures. It may not translate word for word. More like… instructions for producing a pattern of sound and touch associated with the Ship.”

Mira nodded as if this were reasonable village housekeeping. “Then we should try.”

Riya blinked. “Try what?”

“The pattern.”

“Now?”

“Why not now? You scholars always want another month of caution after the evidence has already walked into your kitchen.”

Riya laughed from sheer nerves. “Because reenacting poorly understood ritual sequences connected to a millennia-old orbital anomaly is not standard methodology.”

“No,” Mira said. “But listening is.”

Word spread, though no one admitted to spreading it. That evening a small group gathered in Mira’s courtyard: Devaki with her mourning crescents, Bheema the weaver, Harun the widower, two sisters from the next lane whose grandmother had been a singer, and a boy of fourteen who had no right to stay up so late but remained anyway under pretense of carrying stools. They were not a ceremonial assembly. No incense, no drums, no special dress. The informality itself steadied Riya. If this became nothing, it would at least become nothing honestly.

The sky had cleared after sunset. The Ship hung high, dark against a spill of stars.

Riya brought the seal impressions, the notebook copy, and a shallow bowl of soot paste Mira had mixed “for memory’s sake.” No one proposed using needles. Instead Mira pressed the guarded seal lightly onto Riya’s upper shoulder with the paste. The cool wetness made her shiver. On the left shoulder she stamped the paired motif from the observatory panel. On her sternum, over clothing, she traced the ladder mark with a fingertip. Then she did the same, more faintly, on her own skin where old tattoos already mapped forgotten routes.

“Why me?” Riya asked.

“Because the notebook came through your line,” Mira said. “And because you are the one foolish enough to think hesitation is protection.”

The group stood in a loose crescent facing northward where the cape would be if the hills and night were stripped away. Riya read from her notes, then stopped. Reading aloud felt wrong. Instead she followed the instructions as reconstructed: touch left shoulder, pause on three closed-mouth counts, release at the throat, draw upward with the breath, sustain, cut, descend to sternum, reverse.

The first attempt was awkward. Their voices frayed into self-consciousness. A goat bleated from somewhere behind the wall. The fourteen-year-old nearly giggled and was silenced by his sister’s elbow.

Again.

On the second attempt, Mira corrected the pauses by tapping Riya’s wrist. Closed, closed, closed, open. Ladder rise. Hook turn. Breath held not as strain but as shaping. The resulting sound was not song and not speech in any ordinary sense. It moved between voiced hum, aspirated click, and open-throated tone. A pattern of approach and release.

Again.

By the fourth repetition the courtyard quieted around the act. Crickets resumed their pulse in the intervals. The Ship remained what it had always been: distant, mute, unlowering. Yet Riya felt, not response, but coherence. The sequence settled into the body with unnerving familiarity, as though some part of her nervous system recognized the architecture before her conscious mind did. Left shoulder, hold, throat release, rise. A language not of propositions but of relation—body to sky, lineage to silence, memory to continuity.

When they finished, no one spoke immediately.

Then Bheema said, very softly, “My grandmother used to hush us with that cadence.”

Devaki touched the fading crescents on her shoulder. “And in mourning houses they traced this when the dead could not be named.”

Harun frowned upward. “So all these years we asked why the Ship says nothing. Maybe the old people were not asking it to speak.”

Mira gave him a look of fond exasperation. “Men always imagine silence is withholding.”

The boy, unable to contain himself, blurted, “Did anything happen?”

Riya looked at the dark crescent in the sky. No beam, no tremor, no heavenly descent. Nothing that would satisfy newspaper headlines or scientific committees hungry for anomaly. The mystery of the Ship endured with perfect indifference.

And yet.

“Yes,” she said. “Something happened.”

Later, after the others left and the soot marks had dried faintly against her skin, Riya sat alone in the courtyard with her notebook. She tried to articulate what had changed. Not the Ship. Not even the external facts, though those were substantial enough. She now had evidence that the Thuri tattoo tradition preserved a complex system linking bodily marking, stamp-seals, textile motifs, mnemonic notation, and performative vocal sequences. This system bore family transmission through Vaarta houses and may encode a lost or transformed register of island language associated with ritual practices around the ever-silent Ship. Any one of those claims, argued carefully, could reshape scholarship on ThuLadvipa.

But scholarship was not the deepest shift.

All her life she had inherited a narrative of fracture: family gone from village, language worn down into accent, rituals thinned into anecdotes, tattoos surviving only as museum photographs and the occasional tourist design stripped of context. Heritage, in that narrative, was a ruin to be documented before it disappeared. What she had found in Purja was not unbroken continuity—nothing so sentimental. Loss was real; forgetting was real; violence had done its work. Yet beneath the erosion lay a different model. Not a dead archive, but a distributed one. Skin, cloth, carved post, wedding hem, mourning gesture, grandmother’s prohibition, mother’s half-memory, village correction, shoulder tracing under stars. Pieces had survived in relation, waiting less for discovery than for re-connection.

The next weeks passed in labor both exhilarating and exhausting. Riya formalized consent procedures with a care bordering on ritual itself. She recorded oral histories only where invited, and every recording remained stored with copies in Purja, not solely at the Institute. She taught the younger villagers how to photograph and catalogue their own heirlooms so the record would not leave them entirely. Mira insisted on appearing in exactly none of the official portrait photographs but in all practical discussions. “Let the papers note ‘village interlocutor,’” she said. “I know what my neighbors call me.”

Riya traveled briefly to the north cape observatory ruins with a district guide and returned with better photographs of the carved panel. There, half-buried in lichen, she found a broken stone basin bearing seal impressions around the rim. Not offerings bowl, she thought at once. More likely paste preparation vessel. The ritual and the archive had always been one.

Her report to the Institute began as a conventional survey and gradually became something else. She refrained from grand claims of “decipherment.” She wrote instead of a “multimodal notation complex” embedded in Thuri body-marking traditions and transmitted through Vaarta-associated lineages. She documented correspondences among seals, tattoo motifs, textile borders, carved stone, and notebook glosses. She argued that prior scholarship had reduced these forms to iconography by ignoring positional syntax and performative context. She noted the relation to “sky-listening” sequences associated in oral histories with the silent orbital Ship, while carefully declining to speculate on extraterrestrial communication in any vulgar sense.

Her department head called after reading the first draft. “Riya,” he said, struggling between excitement and academic caution, “do you understand what this could mean?”

“Yes,” she said. “It means we will need to proceed slowly and with the village’s control over publication.”

A pause. “Of course.”

“And the seals do not leave Purja.”

Another pause, longer this time. “We can discuss custodial arrangements—”

“No,” Riya said. “We can discuss reproductions.”

Mira, hearing only Riya’s side of the call, nodded approval from the veranda.

Her mother came to Purja at the end of the monsoon. She stepped from the jeep in a pressed cotton sari wholly unsuited to village mud and promptly sank one sandal into it. Mira laughed so hard she had to sit down. The two women, who had not seen each other in years, embraced with the awkward force of relatives whose affection has spent too long armored by old choices.

Riya watched them in the courtyard beneath the peepal leaves, the Ship a pale hook above the afternoon clouds.

That evening Amma opened the lacquered box and touched each seal in turn, not possessively, but with the astonishment of someone meeting objects from her own dream. “I thought perhaps I had invented them,” she said.

“You invented many things,” Mira replied. “These are not among them.”

They spoke late into the night—of Lata, of the move to the mainland, of what had been hidden for safety and what had been hidden out of habit. Riya listened more than she joined, hearing for the first time how family history thickened when no one was trying to flatten it into explanation. Her mother admitted she remembered fragments of tracing sequences on her own childhood skin before baths. Mira admitted she had assumed Lata had abandoned all interest in village matters. Each had been wrong in ways both ordinary and tender.

Before sleeping, Amma asked to see the soot-stamp pattern on Riya’s shoulder. Riya had begun wearing temporary impressions while working through the notation, not as costume but as method. She turned lamplight toward the mark. Her mother traced the edge without touching. “My mother used this one when storms cut the ferry lines,” she murmured. “She said it kept households speaking to one another in order.”

“In order?” Riya asked.

“So panic would not scatter names.”

There it was again: not magic, not message from above, but social technology braided with ritual. Survival encoded as pattern.

Months later, when the first article draft circulated among a small group of trusted scholars and community reviewers, debate flared exactly as Riya expected. Was this truly language or para-linguistic notation? Did the correspondences demonstrate a script family or a ritual semiotic system? Could one responsibly connect the observatory inscriptions to body-marking practices across centuries? Had scholars been blind, or had evidence simply been too scattered? Riya answered where she could and refused where she should. Some uncertainty, she had learned from Mira, was not failure but fidelity to complexity.

Purja itself changed less dramatically than outsiders would imagine. No flood of tourists arrived, thanks partly to Riya’s fierce gatekeeping and partly to the village’s own talent for appearing unremarkable when necessary. Yet small things shifted. The younger sisters began reweaving border motifs using old sequence logic. Harun’s nephew recorded elders tracing patterns on cloth while reciting associated pauses. The fourteen-year-old, now impossibly proud of having “been there” the night of the courtyard listening, started apprenticing with a calligrapher in town to explore whether the seal patterns could be taught on paper without flattening their bodily origins. Mira pretended to disapprove and secretly supervised everyone.

As for the Ship, it remained.

No signal flashed. No hatch opened. No authoritative explanation descended from the sky to reward renewed human attention. Scientists continued measuring. Pilgrims continued praying. Politicians continued invoking it in speeches when budgets failed. Children continued drawing it in school margins.

But in Purja, and then in a few neighboring villages, people began once more to stand under clear nights and perform certain old listening sequences—not as spectacle, not as certainty, but as remembered alignment. Some used temporary soot stamps, some merely traced the routes on sleeves or skin. The practice spread quietly along kin lines and craft networks, carried less by institutions than by trust. A body archive waking in fragments.

Riya found that her reluctance had not vanished so much as matured. She remained wary of turning living practice into academic possession. She remained suspicious of anyone who wanted the story too neat: lost language solved, family heritage restored, cosmic mystery decoded. None of that was true. What was true was harder and better. A woman sent unwillingly to document old tattoos had discovered that her own family’s silences were part of the very archive she sought. Stamp-seals wrapped in forgotten silk had opened not a solved puzzle but a passageway into relation—between generations, between media, between the island’s enduring skyward question and the human practices built beneath it.

One dry-season evening, nearly a year after her first arrival, Riya climbed the low ridge above Purja where the tamarind trees thinned and the village roofs showed as scattered rust and tile among green. She carried no notebook for once. Only one of the replica seals cast for teaching, warm from her pocket. Below, she could hear faintly the evening sounds—cooking vessels, someone calling cattle home, a loom at last clacking again in tentative rhythm. Above, the Ship held its ancient station, immense and unknowable and utterly quiet.

Riya stood there a long time.

She thought of the phrase in her grandmother’s notebook: no answer expected.

For years she would have read that as resignation, perhaps even defeat. Now she understood it differently. Not all acts of address are bargains. Not all listening is demand. Some patterns endure because they keep a people coherent in the presence of what exceeds explanation. The Ship’s silence had become, over centuries, the island’s great pressure and great canvas. Around it grew fear, theology, science, fraud, art, opportunism, wonder. And among the Thuri, among the Vaarta lines and their broken descendants, there had also grown a discipline: to mark, to remember, to trace relation on skin so that silence did not become amnesia.

She pressed the replica seal into her palm until the carved handle bit lightly.

Behind her, on the path, came the sound of someone approaching with deliberate steps. She turned to see her mother climbing the ridge, breathing hard and refusing assistance on principle.

“You always choose hills at the end of the day,” Amma said when she reached her.

“It helps me think.”

“It makes me regret raising a scholar.”

They stood together facing the sky.

After a while her mother said, “Do you ever wish it would finally speak? The Ship.”

Riya considered. Below them, Purja was lighting its evening lamps one by one. The first bats veered through the dusk. On her own skin, hidden under her sleeve, the faint soot pattern from that afternoon’s work had almost entirely worn away.

“No,” she said at last. “Or not in the way I used to.”

Her mother nodded as if this too were an answer she had expected from the beginning.

The Ship said nothing. The island breathed beneath it. And in that breathing—in silk-wrapped heirlooms, in old women’s forearms, in the pressure of seals remembering what skin had forgotten, in the slow reassembly of a language that had never fully died so much as withdraw into touch—Riya heard, for the first time in her life, not mystery solved, but inheritance answering.
