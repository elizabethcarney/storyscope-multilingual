At low tide the rocks below Castle Eiren shone like black teeth, and beyond them the western sea breathed in long, deliberate sighs, as if some immense creature slept just under the skin of the water. Prince Alaric had loved that sound as a child. Now, in the late summer of his twenty-third year, he went to the cliffs because it was the one place in his father’s kingdom where no one spoke to him of vows.

The kingdom spoke of little else.

Peace with the north had been purchased not in gold but in flesh: Alaric was to wed Lady Yselle of Dunmark before the winter storms. Her dowry was a river valley; her hand would bind two old enemies into one bloodline. Every councilor praised the wisdom of it. Every priest called it sacred. Every servant smiled too carefully when her name was mentioned.

Alaric had seen her three times.

When she was a child, fever had eaten one side of her face and left it puckered and pale, pulling her mouth into a permanent, lopsided severity. One eye watered incessantly. Her right hand, too, was twisted at the wrist. Yet there was nothing monstrous in her manner. She spoke gently, with a grave intelligence, and once, when he had fumbled a formal courtesy, she spared him by turning the awkwardness into a joke at her own expense. That had nearly made him hate her more. Had she been vain or foolish, cruelty would have been easy. Instead she was merely marked, and he, seeing her, felt a recoil so immediate and bodily that shame followed always a beat too late.

He despised himself for it. He despised, more secretly, the kingdom for asking him to master what in him refused mastery.

So he came to the sea.

At dusk the horizon dissolved into silver smoke. The tide drew itself in, then loosed itself over the stones. Alaric stood with his hands clasped behind his back and imagined, not for the first time, another life: some radiant bride from a song, some face that would make desire and duty one thing instead of two.

“Prince.”

The voice seemed less heard than borne upward in the foam.

He started. There, waist-deep among the waves, was a woman he had not seen emerge. She stood where no footing should have held her, the water folding around her like silk around a body. Her skin had the pale, secret luster of the inside of a shell. Her hair floated behind her in a heavy drift, phosphorescent as moonlit plankton, green-white and softly burning in the dark.

He thought first: apparition. Then: temptation.

“Who are you?” he asked.

She did not answer in words. She only tilted her head and smiled—not broadly, but with a strange, knowing softness, as though she recognized in him something he had not spoken aloud. Her eyes were dark as tide pools at midnight.

She lifted one hand and beckoned.

He should have gone. Instead he climbed down the path to the shingle, boots slipping on loose stone. By the time he reached the shore, she had come nearer. The hem of the sea touched his feet. She extended her hand. It was cool, faintly luminous, webbed only by the glisten of water between elegant fingers.

“Are you of the Fair Folk?” he asked.

Her smile altered. Not denial. Not assent. The old stories named beings like her undines, daughters of the deeps, hungry for breath, for souls, for love, for ruin—depending on who told the tale.

He took her hand.

That first touch was enough. The court, the chapel, Lady Yselle’s damaged face, his father’s lined impatience—all of it seemed to fall away as if the sea itself had drawn a curtain of mist between him and the land. She led him into the shallows until the water soaked his clothes to the thigh. She laughed then, soundless, only a shimmer in her throat and shoulders, and with her free hand she touched his cheek as if blessing him.

He met her again the next evening, and the next.

Soon there was no need of summons. At dusk he went to the cove beneath the castle, and she rose to him from the waves. He called her Nerina because she had no name he could hear. She spoke with gesture, with gaze, with the flex of her mouth and hands, and he found in that silence an intoxication deeper than speech. Nothing in her reproached him. Nothing in her reminded him of treaties or dynasties. She was all beauty, all novelty, all the dangerous sweetness of a world before law.

At first she remained in the water. Then, one moonless night, she came ashore.

The instant both feet touched dry sand, her body changed—not in shape, but in bearing. Her shoulders drew tight. Her breath caught. She took one halting step toward him and nearly fell. Alaric caught her, and she clung to him with a force that startled him.

“Does it hurt?” he whispered.

Her face answered. The lovely serenity fractured. Around her eyes gathered a strain so naked it resembled terror. Yet she nodded yes when he asked if she wished to stay.

Each step inland cost her. He felt it in the trembling of her legs beneath his hands, saw it in the whiteness around her mouth. It was as though the very earth were made of knives and she had chosen to walk on their points for him. Still she came. They met in a ruined watchtower hidden among bent pines above the cove, where the sea could still be seen between the stones. There she lay beside him on old moss and neglected rushes, her phosphorescent hair dimming and brightening with her breath. She touched his throat, his chest, his lips, with an almost reverent wonder, as though he were the strange creature and she the scholar of forbidden things.

He called it love because he had no other word large enough.

His ocean of love, he told her once, pressing his forehead to hers. He meant the boundlessness of it, the rush, the overwhelming. She smiled sadly at that, and he did not understand why.

Weeks passed. The banns were read in the chapel. Tailors measured him. Envoys arrived from Dunmark. His father summoned him with increasing coldness. Alaric began to come later to the cove, then to leave sooner. Nerina still rose to meet him, and still, when she crossed onto land, that silent knifepoint agony wrote itself over her features. Yet something had altered. The first wild novelty had thinned. What had seemed an escape now required concealment, explanation, invention. Even beauty, seen nightly, became familiar. And familiarity, in Alaric’s ungenerous heart, began to make room again for duty.

The truth did not strike him all at once. It ebbed.

One evening he looked at her while she leaned against the tower wall, breathing shallowly from the pain of being there, and thought—not cruelly, not consciously at first—of winter stores, of the northern levy, of Lady Yselle’s father and his six thousand men. The thought passed, but it left a line in the sand.

Another evening he imagined scandal, war, his mother’s tears. Another, he noticed how Nerina watched the sea between the pines as if listening to a call older than he was.

At last, in the gray hour before rain, he said, “I cannot.”

She stood very still.

“I am promised,” he continued, and heard the feebleness of it even as he spoke. Promised: as though he had not spent these weeks unstitching promise after promise in secret. “The kingdom—there are obligations. I thought…” He stopped. What had he thought? That desire could suspend consequence? That tides did not turn?

Nerina did not accuse him. That was worst of all.

She stepped toward him, and pain flickered across her face with the familiar blade-thin intensity. Then she laid her hand over his heart. Her palm was cold. She held it there until his pulse steadied under it, as if she were returning something he had borrowed recklessly. When she withdrew, a faint salt damp remained on his skin.

He reached for her then, belatedly, but she had already turned away.

The wedding to Lady Yselle was held in the first week of winter. Snow powdered the battlements. Bells rang over a kingdom relieved to be safe. Yselle stood beside him in cloth-of-silver, her damaged face uncovered, her one clear eye steady. When she placed her twisted hand in his, he felt not desire but a grave, surprising respect. It was not love. Perhaps it might become a kind of tenderness. Kingdoms, he was learning, were often built on lesser and more durable things.

Years later, people said he had become a good king.

Yet sometimes, when storms struck the western coast and the surf burned green at night, Alaric would wake with the taste of salt in his mouth and think of a woman who had walked on knives for his brief astonishment.

As for Nerina, no chapel ever named her ending.

Fishermen told of seeing, after tempests, a figure in the spray atop the reef line, hair streaming with cold fire. Sailors swore that a singer without a voice guided lost boats clear of the rocks, then vanished before dawn. And children on the shore, daring one another at twilight, said there was an undine in the tide pools who no longer rose for princes, who had learned the mutable law of hearts and chosen at last to belong only to the sea.

Perhaps she dissolved into it wholly, becoming wave, foam, phosphor, salt.

Or perhaps she remained herself and simply ceased to mistake hunger for devotion.

In either case, when the tide withdrew from Castle Eiren, it left behind what all oceans leave: bright fragments, a line of wreckage, and something smoothed into a new shape by the endless, indifferent mercy of return.
